Sparse autoencoders do not guarantee local features

A frozen SAE can produce exactly the same feature activations, yet require different input variables to explain them. How can we measure that difference?

One small example

The input map changes, not the SAE

Under the identity map, z₁ = x₁ and z₂ = x₂. Under XOR mixing, z₁ = x₁ ⊕ x₂ and z₂ = x₂.

In both cases the joint multiset of feature codes is {00, 01, 10, 11}. Under XOR mixing, predicting z₁ perfectly needs both x₁ and x₂. Predicting z₂ still needs only x₂. Thus exact scope cost rises from 2 to 3.

These are illustrative deterministic activations of a fixed SAE under two bijective input assignments.

Definitions

Optimal input scope curve

Let zⱼ(x) be activation of feature j in a frozen SAE. Choose a scope Aⱼ of controlled inputs for each feature, paying one unit per selected input.

B(A) = Σⱼ |Aⱼ|

The best predictor of zⱼ from xAⱼ is E[zⱼ | xAⱼ]. Its omitted variance is E[(zⱼ − E[zⱼ | xAⱼ])²].

Lz(B) = minB(A) ≤ B Σⱼ E[(zⱼ − E[zⱼ | xAⱼ])²]

In the two-bit example, uniform binary variables each have variance 0.25. An XOR feature is unpredictable until both contributing factors are included in its scope.

Mathematical result

What the exact construction shows

On four input factors over the finite field F₇, different bijective maps preserve the same representation sample distribution and the same frozen SAE. Their interaction order ranges from 1 to 4.

The budget needed for exact localization scales in direct proportion to that order. The construction also covers a setting where sparse coding uniquely selects the common dictionary.

This proves that an SAE reconstruction and sparsity objective alone cannot guarantee local input dependence.

Additional compatibility and spectral results bound localization cost using joint input effects and decoder directions.

~35 s

Talk track

  1. The paper gives an exact construction, not just a toy example. Four finite-field input factors are remapped by different interaction orders. The frozen SAE and the representation distribution remain the same.
  2. Yet the exact input-scope cost grows in exact proportion to interaction order, from one times to four times the baseline. This rules out locality as a consequence of sparse reconstruction alone.

Experiment design

Five frozen representation–SAE pairs

For each independent run, a small representation model and a 24-to-48 overcomplete SAE were trained and then frozen. The evaluation uses the complete set of 256 Boolean input states.

The correspondence between inputs and codes is varied with identity, three-factor XOR and five-factor XOR maps.

At 90% explained activation variance, the mean budgets across the five SAEs are 136.0, 192.4, and 202.8 respectively, increases of 41.5% and 49.1% for the two XOR maps.

Paired-input identity

Estimating scope error without a full grid

Draw independent inputs x and x′ from a product intervention distribution. For a proposed subset A, form y = (xA, x′−A).

E[(zⱼ(x) − E[zⱼ | xA])²] = ½ E[(zⱼ(x) − zⱼ(y))²]

Estimate each candidate scope's omitted variance with paired interventions. Optimize the selection of scopes under the total budget constraint to estimate the curve.

The five-SAE experiment reports mean maximum normalized curve error of 0.123, 0.0472, 0.0216, and 0.0109 for 32, 128, 512, and 2,048 pairs per scope, respectively.

~45 s

Talk track

  1. How can we estimate the optimal scope curve? Fix the inputs within a proposed scope. Draw another independent input configuration for the factors outside the scope. The squared difference of the two feature activations, divided by two, measures the omitted conditional variance.
  2. The more pairs we sample, the closer the empirical curve gets to the exact curve. Compare 32 pairs with 2,048 pairs; the reported mean maximum normalized error drops from 0.123 to 0.0109.
